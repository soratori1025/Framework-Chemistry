\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The results support an evidence-guided approach to molecular property modelling, not a universal ranking of model families. Across five independent endpoints, descriptor-based, graph-based, and hybrid predictors each had tasks on which they performed well. Adding the 210 RDKit descriptors changed performance in the same direction in two independently written implementations on four of the five tasks, and by a similar amount: a clear gain on FreeSolv and a change of a few percent or less elsewhere. On ESOL the two disagreed: Chemprop's hybrid was better than graph-only on nine of ten splits and collapsed on the tenth, and retraining that configuration on that split spans $0.75$ to $6.12$ RMSE across six seeds, so the disagreement is about the stability of one configuration rather than about the endpoint. Both failures are removed by bounding the same descriptor block, and the extreme value the audit reports is an \cfg{Ipc} value $3114$ training standard deviations outside the training range; both are visible only because the workflow keeps per-split and per-run records rather than a summary.

It is worth stating what this means for the chemist who has one dataset in hand. The default ``use the graph'' or ``use all descriptors'' habit is not wrong, but it is a guess, and the cost of testing it is now small. On phenacetin's endpoint the same graph encoder gave a test RMSE of $0.681$ without descriptors and $0.595$ with them on that split, a difference of $0.086$; averaged over ten splits the graph-only and full-descriptor means are within $0.001$ RMSE of each other on ESOL ($0.623$ versus $0.623$), which is exactly why a single split should not be used as a decision rule. The same holds for compact routes: the recommender gave the lowest mean on Lipophilicity ($0.581$ versus $0.591$ for random subsets), although the paired difference did not survive correction, and it did not separate from the control on any other endpoint. None of these numbers is a universal rule, and the framework deliberately does not turn any of them into one. What it does is make the alternatives cheap enough to compare on the user's own data, with the same split and the same record.

Two different questions are entangled in a comparison between software packages, and they should be separated. The first is whether descriptors help a graph model at all; the unconfounded estimate of that is the within-system contrast, graph only versus the same encoder with descriptors, which requires no second implementation. The second is whether that effect is a property of the endpoint or an accident of one codebase; that is a replication question, and it is answered by an independently written implementation showing the same sign and roughly the same size. Our design answers the second question and supports the inference by the first: the within-system contrasts show the effect, and the agreement with Chemprop v2.3.1, which differs in encoder, featurisation, optimiser and training loop, shows that the effect survives a change of code. It is not an architecture-controlled decomposition, and we do not read it as one. The price of that reading is paid in the Methods: the descriptor blocks exported from the two environments agree in 99.87\% of cells, so the two systems saw effectively, but not exactly, the same input.

The contribution this paper claims is correspondingly narrow and, we think, useful. It is not a new predictor or a new state-of-the-art selector. It is the combination of (i) an audit that turns data problems and descriptor evidence into inspectable artefacts before any model is fitted, (ii) a configuration layer in which the same split, checkpoint rule and evaluation produce the alternatives, and (iii) a ten-split record in which a divergent run cannot hide inside a mean. The audit found duplicates in two of five benchmarks, one of them with conflicting labels, and the scorecard showed that training-partition evidence can produce a compact candidate set---though on ESOL the two smallest subsets scored worse than the full 210-descriptor pool, which is the honest limit of the rule. What neither tool does is name the important descriptors: the recommender is a candidate-generation heuristic built on a fixed relevance-and-redundancy rule, it ranked fourth of six selectors, and a block test over the 20 dataset--budget cells, while rejecting equality of the selectors, does not separate it from any individual selector, including the best. Its value is that the proposal is compact, stable across splits (mean Jaccard 0.76) and explainable feature by feature.

For a reader who wants a rule rather than a framework, the work supports a short checklist with explicit thresholds. Before fitting: curate duplicated structures whose labels conflict, because a scaffold split can place the same structure on both sides; in these five benchmarks that affected BBBP (64 duplicated structures, 11 with conflicting labels) and, without label conflicts, ESOL. Before interpreting a score: check how many descriptors lie outside the training range for the test molecules ($6.3\%$ of test molecules on ESOL, $3.8\%$ on BBBP) and, more importantly, the largest standardised value they produce, because a maximum in the hundreds or thousands of training standard deviations means the score describes extrapolation rather than validation; ESOL split 3 reaches $3114$ and is the split on which two routes diverged. Before choosing a representation: run at least graph only, the full descriptor block and one compact block on the same splits, and report per-split values, because on these endpoints the best choice differed between tasks and the margins were often a few hundredths of a metric unit. These thresholds are conventions drawn from this study, not validated cut-offs, and a prospective application should re-derive them.

Several limitations remain. Ten split manifests still provide limited statistical power for differences of a few hundredths of a metric unit, and overlapping training sets make paired intervals descriptive rather than definitive; the permutation tests reported in Additional file 1 agree with the corrected intervals in 175 of 184 comparisons, but they do not correct for that overlap and are correspondingly more permissive. Hyperparameters were fixed a priori rather than tuned. Two one-factor sensitivity analyses are reported in Additional file 1: varying the encoder width over 64--256 leaves the sign of the descriptor effect unchanged and changes its size between roughly 7\% and 19\% (Table S21), and varying the redundancy threshold over 0.70--0.90 changes the selected descriptor set substantially (Jaccard 0.51--0.77 against the default) while the downstream score moves by at most 0.024 RMSE (Table S20). Settings not varied here, such as the size of the downstream forest or the learning rate, could change the size of the small effects; tuning was not performed, so the reported values characterise a fixed protocol rather than an optimal one. The descriptor blocks were generated by different RDKit releases in the two environments, which affects 0.13\% of descriptor cells. The primary blood-brain barrier benchmark retained duplicate structures, including groups with conflicting labels, and the audit found that tautomeric representations can produce different scaffold strings for the same InChIKey. The duplicate-controlled analysis addresses sensitivity to curation, but changes to data composition and splits prevent a causal attribution to leakage alone. The descriptor-only reference and the framework's direct descriptor model were produced by different runners. The experiments use two-dimensional inputs, five endpoints, and retrospective splits; they do not test prospective use, multi-task training, or three-dimensional and mixture inputs, for which the framework exposes interfaces but no evidence. A prospective benchmark in which the audit and the route choices are fixed before the test data are unblinded would be the natural next step, together with extensions to other endpoints and to duplicate-aware partitioning.
